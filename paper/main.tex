\begin{abstract}
Post-quantum migration planning increasingly uses learned rankers scored against rule-based
doctrine heuristics. A prior Q-Trust study reported an ``honest tie'': a graph neural network
attains tie-aware Kendall $\tb=0.7168$ versus a doctrine baseline at $0.7210$ on 42
host-disjoint cryptographic-bill-of-materials (CBOM) estates (zero wins, 41 ties, one loss),
and concluded that model superiority is not measurable. We show that this tie is not evidence
of parity: it is the maximum score any ranker can attain. Because tie-aware $\tb$ discards
tied pairs, its attainable ceiling is $\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$, where $\distinct$ is the
number of distinguishable pairs. On the 42 estates, 401 of 803 pairs are tied and only 402 are
distinguishable, so the ceiling is $0.721024$---exactly the doctrine score. The network orders
401 of 402 distinguishable pairs correctly (99.75\%); its entire shortfall is one inversion. A
parameter-free key-size rule and a seven-parameter logistic model attain the ceiling on 42/42
folds; a six-parameter ridge model reproduces the network's score on all 42 folds, including
its single failure. A field audit explains why: all 42 estates have zero dependency edges and
constant criticality, vendor readiness, and deadline; every asset is classical; the doctrine
formula algebraically reduces to a monotone function of key size. Removing all 2,000 synthetic
training graphs leaves $\tb$ unchanged on 42/42 folds. A clean warm-start control
(synthetic-only initialization, $n_{\text{real}}=0$) places the network $0.120$ below the
doctrine ($p=0.0018$), so the released parity is partly an artifact of initialization
exposure. A dependency-rich positive control raises the ceiling to $0.999$ and shows the metric
can discriminate, yet the network still does not beat a doctrine blind to multi-hop reach. We
re-evaluate the released discovery checkpoint and obtain F1 $0.9395$, not the reported
$0.9525$, under a $76.7\%$ positive prior (majority F1 $0.868$). We contribute a ceiling
theorem for tie-aware rank evaluation; a taxonomy of rank metrics by tie-sensitivity
(tie-aware $\tb=\gamma\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$, where $\gamma$ is Goodman--Kruskal's
association, so the same ordering scores $1.000$, $0.721$, $0.807$, and $0.533$ under $\gamma$,
$\tb$, $\rho$, and $\tau_a$); a labelling theorem that prices the remedy (bisecting the largest
tied group yields $m^2/4$ new distinguishable pairs for $m$ labels, so the ceiling is cheapest to
repair where ties are densest); an eight-mode construct-validity checklist; a claim ledger; and a
reporting standard: report the ceiling, the number of effective pairs, and attainment---not just
$\tb$. We further show that cross-corpus checkpoint comparisons are routinely contaminated: on one
fixed held-out split, two earlier checkpoints had trained on $87.6\%$ and $92.2\%$ of the evaluation
files, so only the zero-overlap checkpoint's F1 ($0.8966$) is interpretable, and neither more epochs
nor more data raises it---consistent with a ceiling set by label noise. On designed estates the ceiling is governed by tie concentration rather than estate size
($0.43$ at $90\%$ key-size monoculture for $n$ from 10 to 5{,}000), and about two expert labels
per asset suffice to raise it to $0.90$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The transition to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is no longer a forecast; it is a dated
obligation. Executive Order~14412 of 22 June 2026 directs US federal agencies to transition
high-value assets and high-impact systems to PQC for digital signatures by 31 December 2031
and for key establishment by 31 December 2030~\cite{eo14412}. For national-security systems,
CNSS Policy~15 requires CNSA~2.0 algorithms by the same horizon~\cite{cnssp15}. Section~5(d) of
the order further requires guidance on the \emph{minimum elements of a cryptographic bill of
materials} (CBOM) within 270 days---approximately 19 March 2027---whose elements must enable
``the automated assessment of the cryptographic assets''~\cite{eo14412}. Automated assessment
requires knowing which assets exist, which are at risk, and \emph{in what order} to replace
them. The first two have maturing tooling~\cite{cyclonedx,ibm_cbom,hess2026,cryptoscope}; the
third does not.

The deployment data sharpen the problem. Across more than two billion TLS handshakes to one
million domains from eleven vantage points, default hybrid post-quantum key exchange rose from
31.3\% to 49.2\% between July 2025 and March 2026, but \emph{no post-quantum signature
algorithm was ever negotiated}, and jurisdictions with \emph{earlier} deadlines showed
\emph{lower} adoption (32.4\% versus 45.7\%)~\cite{mindthegap2026}. Organizations are making
real ordering decisions under a real deadline, with no validated tooling and no ground-truth
oracle.

A natural way to automate prioritization is to learn a ranking function over CBOM assets and
compare it with an interpretable doctrine heuristic. Q-Trust is an open pipeline built for this
purpose: a polyglot scanner emits CycloneDX~1.7 CBOMs, a graph neural network (GNN) ranks
assets for migration, and an attestation layer records completed
work~\cite{qtrust_repo}. Its planning study evaluated a GNN on 42 host-disjoint CBOM estates
(277 hosts) with leave-one-estate-out (LOO) training. The headline result was a tie: the GNN
reached mean tie-aware Kendall $\tb=0.7168$, the doctrine reached $0.7210$, and the fold-level
outcome was 0 wins, 41 ties, and 1 loss. The authors interpreted this as evidence that
``better than the doctrine'' is not currently a measurable claim, and published the tie
deliberately rather than tuning until the model won.

This paper re-examines that conclusion. The tie is real, and the measurement is honest, but the
interpretation is not licensed by the artifact. On these estates the target ordering is almost
entirely tied: 401 of the 803 pairs are pairs of assets with identical priority. Tie-aware
$\tb$ discards those pairs, so the metric's denominator contains only 402 effective
comparisons. The attainable ceiling is therefore $\sqrt{402/803}=0.721024$. That number is the
doctrine score. The GNN is not ``tied with'' the doctrine in the sense of two methods
converging on a difficult problem; it is at the ceiling, one pair short, on a problem that
contains almost no decision information beyond a single feature.

The mechanism is visible in the data. Every one of the 42 estates has zero dependency edges.
Criticality is \texttt{medium} for all 277 assets, vendor readiness is unrecorded for all 277,
and the deadline-pressure term is constant within each estate. Every asset is classical. Under
these conditions the doctrine formula
\begin{equation}
p_i = 2c_i + 1.5v_i + \frac{\log(1+k_i)}{4}
      + \frac{\delta\,c_i}{5} - 0.3\,\mathrm{indeg}_i
\label{eq:doctrine}
\end{equation}
(where $c_i$ is criticality, $v_i$ vendor readiness, $k_i$ key size, $\delta$ deadline pressure,
and $\mathrm{indeg}_i$ in-degree) collapses to
\begin{equation}
p_i = 4 + 0.4\,\delta + \frac{\log(1+k_i)}{4}.
\label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
The doctrine ordering is exactly descending key size. Removing key size changes the ordering in
37 of 42 estates; removing criticality, vendor readiness, deadline, or dependency edges changes
it in none. A parameter-free key-size rule matches the doctrine on 42/42 folds. A seven-parameter
pairwise logistic model attains the ceiling on 42/42 folds. A six-parameter ridge model
reproduces the GNN's per-fold $\tb$ on all 42 folds, including the one fold where both fall
below the ceiling. The GNN's 237{,}070 parameters buy nothing that a linear model with six
features does not already have.

The shortfall is equally small. Of the 402 distinguishable pairs, the GNN orders 401 correctly;
the one error is a single cross-group inversion in \texttt{real\_gov\_edu\_2.json}, where the
ceiling is $0.5345$ and the model scores $0.3563$ (5 of 6 pairs). Across all folds, pooled pair
accuracy is $401/402=99.75\%$. On a repacked 38-estate cohort (275 hosts, all but two of which
also appear in the 42-estate cohort), the GNN attains the ceiling on 36/38 folds and orders 458
of 461 distinguishable pairs correctly. Removing all 2,000 synthetic training graphs reproduces
every fold's $\tb$ to $10^{-6}$.

These results do not show that the GNN is a bad model. They show that the benchmark, as
constructed, cannot distinguish a good model from a key-size sort. That is a construct-validity
failure, and it is the subject of this paper. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A ceiling theorem for tie-aware rank evaluation.} For a strict prediction and a
tied target preorder, $\tb\le\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$, with equality iff every distinguishable
pair is concordant (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}). The ceiling is a property of the label, not the
model.
\item \textbf{A taxonomy of rank metrics by tie-sensitivity.} We show $\tb=\gamma\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$,
where $\gamma=(C-D)/\distinct$ is Goodman--Kruskal's association (Somers' $D$ for strict
predictions), so the ``attainment'' of the ceiling is a standard statistic. $\tau_a$, $\tb$, and
Spearman $\rho$ are tie-fragile (ceiling below one); $\gamma$, Somers' $D$, NDCG@$k$, and
average precision are tie-robust (ceiling one) (Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}). On the real corpus
the same ordering scores $1.000$ ($\gamma$), $0.721$ ($\tb$), $0.807$ ($\rho$), and $0.533$
($\tau_a$)---the metric choice alone decides whether the result looks like saturation or
failure (Section~\ref{sec:metricverdict}).
\item \textbf{A measurement of the Q-Trust planning result against that ceiling.} The 42-fold
campaign has $\nzero=803$, $\distinct=402$, and ceiling $0.721024$; the doctrine is at the
ceiling by construction, and the GNN is one pair short (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A field audit and algebraic explanation.} Zero edges, constant risk fields, and
all-classical assets reduce the doctrine to key-size sorting; a leave-one-term-out ablation and
a parameter-free baseline confirm it (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}).
\item \textbf{Transparent baselines and controls.} A seven-parameter logistic attains the
ceiling on 42/42 folds; a six-parameter ridge matches the GNN on 42/42 folds; a zero-shot
checkpoint scores $0.604$; zero-synthetic training leaves $\tb$ unchanged; a clean warm start
exposes the initialization confound (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A repacked-cohort replication and a positive control.} The 38-estate repacking
yields 36/38 folds at ceiling; a dependency-rich synthetic control reaches ceiling $0.999$ and
shows the metric can discriminate, while the GNN still fails to beat a doctrine blind to
multi-hop reach (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{An eight-mode construct-validity checklist and a claim ledger.} We distill eight
cheap, independent, mutually concealing failure modes that any audit of this task should check,
and we map every headline number to an artifact with a status: reproduced, reproduced but
vacuous, or not reproduced (Sections~\ref{sec:checklist} and~\ref{sec:artifacts}).
\end{enumerate}

The scope is deliberately narrow. Q-Trust also includes cryptographic-usage discovery,
side-channel experiments, reinforcement-learning sequencing, and on-chain attestation. Those
components are described in Appendix~\ref{app:system} and are not used to support the ranking
claims. The paper's claim is about what the planning benchmark measures, not about whether PQC
migration planning is easy or whether Q-Trust is a useful inventory tool. On the evidence here,
the inventory and attestation layers can be useful; the learned-prioritization claim cannot.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Cryptographic discovery and CBOMs}
Static cryptographic-API analysis has produced mature misuse detectors: CryptoGuard for
Java~\cite{cryptoguard}, CogniCrypt and CrySL for API-usage rules~\cite{cognicrypt,crysl}, and
the CryptoAPI-Bench family for evaluation~\cite{cryptoapibench,afrose2021}. The closest
peer-reviewed \emph{learned} model, Rodrigues et al.~\cite{rodrigues2023}, compares graph
embeddings with SVM/MLP classifiers on Java snippets; its split is a plain 80/20 partition with
no repository disjointness, it reports precision/recall/F1 without confidence intervals, and it
has no threats-to-validity section. These tools answer ``is this API used incorrectly?'' rather
than ``which asset should migrate first?'' CBOM standards~\cite{cyclonedx,ibm_cbom} and the
NCCoE migration project~\cite{nccoe_pqc} define the inventory substrate but not the
prioritization target. Q-Trust's inspector is a usage-and-inventory scanner in this line; its
discovery model is a fine-tuned CodeBERTa checkpoint~\cite{codebert,codeberta}.

\subsection{PQC migration planning}
Recent planning work uses dependency graphs and optimization. Sharma \emph{et al.} formulate
dependency-aware migration planning on simulated enterprise graphs~\cite{sharma2026}; Gharote
\emph{et al.} add budget and schedule constraints~\cite{gharote2026}; Gupta and Mittal propose
a cloud-readiness framework~\cite{gupta2026}; N\"ather and Hirsch study static discovery and
assessment~\cite{nother2026}; Shaw uses an LLM-assisted risk score~\cite{shaw2026}. The recent
MAgiCS workshop~\cite{magics2026} concentrates migration work: Nzetchuen et al.\ survey
frameworks~\cite{nzetchuen2026}, Loebenberger et al.\ formalize migration cost~\cite{loebenberger2026},
and Riou et al.\ document how easily post-quantum signature \emph{benchmarks} mislead through
inconsistent methodology~\cite{riou2026}. These concerns are structural---what must change, in
what order, at what cost---rather than evaluative: none asks how one would know whether a
proposed ordering is correct. None releases a learned ranker evaluated against expert
preference labels on real estates. Q-Trust is, to our knowledge, the first to release a learned
ranker, a doctrine baseline, and the estate artifacts together. That release is what makes the
present audit possible.

\subsection{Evaluation validity and rank metrics}
The failure we document is an instance of benchmark construct validity, not of model capacity.
Kapoor and Narayanan catalog leakage and reproducibility failures across ML-based
science~\cite{kapoor2023}; Geirhos \emph{et al.} describe shortcut learning~\cite{geirhos2020};
Sculley \emph{et al.} describe hidden technical debt in ML systems~\cite{sculley2015}. In
ranking, Kendall's $\tau$~\cite{kendall1938} is a standard ordinal measure, and its tie-aware
variant is appropriate when the target contains genuine ties. But a tie-aware measure has a
ceiling whenever ties exist; reporting the raw value without the ceiling invites the
interpretation that a model ``nearly matches'' a target when it has in fact saturated the
metric. Calibration and selective prediction~\cite{guo2017,geifman2017} are the analogous fixes
for probabilistic classifiers: report the operating regime, not just a scalar. Our ceiling
theorem makes the correction explicit for rank fidelity.

\subsection{The Q-Trust instrument}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the pipeline. The inspector scans source, binaries, and TLS
endpoints and emits CycloneDX~1.7 CBOMs. The risk engine maps algorithms, key sizes, and
compliance rules to a priority score. The planner converts a CBOM to a six-feature graph
(algorithm type, normalized key size, vendor readiness, criticality, deadline, required rate)
and trains a hybrid GCN/GATv2 model~\cite{kipf2017,gatv2} with a listwise ListMLE
loss~\cite{listmle}. The RL sequencer packs estates into migration batches; the attestation
layer records completions in UUPS-upgradeable registries with EIP-712 signatures~\cite{eip712,base}.
The present paper uses only the CBOM-to-graph converter, the doctrine formula, and the LOO
harness. The other components are context, not evidence.

\section{Problem Formulation and the Ceiling Theorem}
\label{sec:ceiling}

\subsection{Estates, features, and target}
An estate $E$ is a set of $n$ assets. Each asset $i$ has a feature vector
$x_i=(\alpha_i, k_i, v_i, c_i, \delta, r_i)$, where $\alpha_i$ is an algorithm-type code, $k_i$
the key size, $v_i\in\{0,1\}$ vendor PQC readiness, $c_i$ criticality, $\delta$ a per-estate
deadline-pressure indicator, and $r_i$ a required-rate feature. A dependency graph $G=(V,A)$
may add edges; in the released TLS CBOMs $A=\varnothing$ (Section~\ref{sec:corpus}). The
doctrine assigns each asset the priority in Eq.~\eqref{eq:doctrine}. The target is not a strict
ranking: assets with equal priority are genuinely tied. Formally, the target is a preorder
$\succeq$ that partitions the $n$ assets into $g$ equivalence classes $G_1,\dots,G_g$; assets in
the same class are interchangeable for migration purposes.

A learned ranker outputs a permutation $\pi$ of the assets (strict, no ties). The evaluation
asks how well $\pi$ agrees with $\succeq$.

\subsection{Tie-aware Kendall $\tb$}
Let $C$ and $D$ be the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs among the $\nzero=n(n-1)/2$
unordered pairs. Let $T_x$ and $T_y$ be the numbers of pairs tied in the prediction and the
target, respectively. Kendall's $\tb$ is
\begin{equation}
\tb(\pi,\succeq)=\frac{C-D}{\sqrt{(\nzero-T_x)(\nzero-T_y)}}.
\label{eq:taub}
\end{equation}
Because $\pi$ is strict, $T_x=0$. Because the target has $g$ classes of sizes $n_1,\dots,n_g$,
$T_y=\sum_j n_j(n_j-1)/2$, and the number of distinguishable pairs is
\begin{equation}
\distinct=\nzero-T_y=\sum_{j<\ell} n_j n_\ell .
\label{eq:d}
\end{equation}
Thus $\tb=(C-D)/\sqrt{\nzero\distinct}$.

\subsection{The ceiling}
\begin{theorem}[Tie-aware ceiling]
For any strict ranking $\pi$ and any target preorder $\succeq$ with $\distinct$ distinguishable
pairs,
\begin{equation}
\tb(\pi,\succeq)\le \sqrt{\frac{\distinct}{\nzero}},
\label{eq:ceiling}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if every distinguishable pair is concordant.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Among the $\distinct$ distinguishable pairs, $C+D=\distinct$, so $C-D\le \distinct$.
Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:taub} with $T_x=0$ and $\nzero-T_y=\distinct$ gives
$\tb\le \distinct/\sqrt{\nzero\distinct}=\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$. Equality holds iff $D=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Saturation is not parity]
If two rankers both attain the ceiling, their $\tb$ values are equal regardless of how they
order assets within tied classes. A doctrine that is a refinement of the target preorder
attains the ceiling by construction.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Effective sample size]
The number of comparisons that can affect $\tb$ is $\distinct$, not $\nzero$. The fraction of
usable pairs is $\distinct/\nzero$, and the ceiling is its square root. Reporting $\tb$ without
$\distinct/\nzero$ hides the effective size of the evaluation.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Attainment]
Define attainment $a=\tb/\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}\in[0,1]$. Then $a=1$ iff all distinguishable
pairs are concordant, and $a$ is the fraction of the ceiling that a ranker achieves. Attainment
is comparable across estates with different tie structures; raw $\tb$ is not.
\end{corollary}

The theorem is elementary, but its consequence is not: on a corpus with 50\% tied pairs, the
maximum possible $\tb$ is about $0.707$. A model at $0.7168$ and a doctrine at $0.7210$ are not
``close'' in any absolute sense; they are at $99.2\%$ and $100\%$ of a ceiling that is fixed by
the label. A benchmark that reports only $\tb$ cannot tell whether a model has room to improve.

\subsection{What would count as evidence of superiority}
The theorem also says what a useful benchmark must do. To license a claim that a learned ranker
beats a doctrine, the evaluation needs (i) a target with many distinguishable pairs, so the
ceiling is well below one; (ii) a doctrine that is not itself the label generator, or at least a
target that includes information the doctrine cannot express; and (iii) a reported ceiling,
effective pair count, and attainment. A tie at the ceiling is evidence about the label, not
about the model. We return to these requirements in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{A taxonomy of rank metrics under ties}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

The ceiling of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ceiling} is specific to $\tb$, but ties affect different rank
metrics differently, and the choice of metric can change the verdict on the same ordering. We
make this precise and show that the quantity we called attainment is a standard statistic.

\begin{definition}[Tie-corrected association]
For a strict prediction $\pi$ and a target preorder with $\distinct$ distinguishable pairs,
\begin{equation}
\gamma=\frac{C-D}{\distinct}.
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:gamma} is Goodman and Kruskal's $\gamma$~\cite{goodman1954}; when the prediction
has no ties it coincides with Somers' $D$~\cite{somers1962}. The following identity is the
reason $\tb$ looks like a near-miss.

\begin{theorem}[Tie decomposition]
\label{thm:decomp}
$\tb = \gamma\,\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\tb=(C-D)/\sqrt{\nzero\distinct}=[(C-D)/\distinct]\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}=\gamma\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Attainment is $\gamma$]
The attainment $a=\tb/\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$ of Corollary~3 is exactly $\gamma$. It is not a
new statistic: it is Goodman--Kruskal's $\gamma$, whose ceiling is $1$ by definition. $\tb$ is
$\gamma$ discounted by the tie factor $\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$.
\end{corollary}

This has two consequences. First, $\gamma$ is \emph{ceiling-free}: a value of $1$ means every
distinguishable pair is concordant, however many pairs are tied. Second, $\tb$ conflates two
quantities---agreement on distinguishable pairs ($\gamma$) and the tie structure
($\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$)---and reporting it alone hides which of the two is low. On the
Q-Trust corpus the tie factor is $\sqrt{402/803}=0.707$; a doctrine at $\gamma=1$ can therefore
never exceed $\tb=0.707$ in pooled terms.

Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} classifies the metrics a benchmark might report. A \emph{tie-fragile}
metric has a ceiling below one: the raw value is bounded away from one by the tie structure, and
the bound must be reported. A \emph{tie-robust} metric has ceiling one: it normalizes by the
distinguishable pairs or by the ideal gain, so saturation does not masquerade as a near-miss.
The classification explains the earlier draft's uninformative top-10 result: NDCG@$k$ and
average precision are tie-robust only when $k$ is smaller than the number of relevant items;
when $k\ge n$, every ordering achieves the ideal gain and the metric is vacuous.

The practical recommendation follows directly. For a tied migration target, report $\gamma$ (or
Somers' $D$) as the primary agreement measure, because its ceiling is one and it is directly
interpretable as the fraction of distinguishable pairs ordered correctly. Report $\tb$ only
alongside its ceiling, and report NDCG@$k$ or AP only with a meaningful $k$. A benchmark that
reports a single tie-fragile number without its ceiling cannot distinguish a saturated model
from a mediocre one.

\section{Corpus and Field Audit}
\label{sec:corpus}

\subsection{The 42-estate cohort}
The primary cohort contains 42 CBOMs, one per estate, covering 277 public TLS certificates.
Each host appears in exactly one estate (277 unique hosts; zero within-cohort repeats), so the
packing is host-disjoint. The estates are small: 37 have seven assets, two have four, one each
has two, three, and five. The mean size is 6.60. The asset composition is RSA-2048 (175),
ECDSA-P256 (89), RSA-4096 (10), RSA-3072 (1), ECDSA-P384 (1), and one asset with a missing key
size. All assets are classical; there are no ML-KEM, ML-DSA, or SLH-DSA assets in the cohort.

These are public endpoint certificates, not enterprise inventories. We therefore call them
``estates'' rather than ``enterprise CBOMs.'' The distinction matters for external validity:
public TLS certificates expose key sizes and expiry dates, but not internal dependencies,
business criticality, or vendor contracts.

\subsection{The 38-estate repacking}
A second cohort, dated 2026-09-18, contains 38 CBOMs covering 275 hosts. It is a re-scan and
repacking of the same population: 275 of its 275 hosts also appear in the 42-estate cohort,
which has two additional hosts. The 38 cohort is therefore not an independent sample; it is a
protocol replication under a different estate packing (32 estates of eight assets, two of five,
two of three, one each of two and one). It tests whether the result depends on the particular
packing; it does not test generalization to new organizations. We report it as such.

\subsection{Field audit}
Table~\ref{tab:audit} summarizes the fields that the doctrine uses. Every estate has zero
dependency edges: the CBOMs do not carry explicit \texttt{depends\_on} links, and the
host-affinity fallback finds no repeated host within an estate. Criticality is \texttt{medium}
for all 277 assets. Vendor readiness is unrecorded for all 277. The deadline is computed once
per estate from the minimum certificate expiry and is therefore constant within an estate. The
only field that varies within an estate is key size.

\subsection{Algebraic collapse}
With $c_i=2$, $v_i=0$, $\mathrm{indeg}_i=0$, and $\delta$ constant within an estate,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:doctrine} becomes Eq.~\eqref{eq:collapse}. The priority is a strictly increasing
function of $\log(1+k_i)$; hence the doctrine ordering is exactly descending key size. This is
not an empirical coincidence or a fitted approximation. It follows from the released values and
the released formula.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} confirms the algebra. Removing the key-size term changes the doctrine
ordering in 37 of 42 estates. Removing criticality, vendor readiness, deadline, or dependency
edges changes it in none. The five estates where removing key size does not change the order are
those in which all assets share a key size (the ordering is then all ties, and any permutation
is equivalent). A parameter-free rule that sorts by key size matches the doctrine on 42/42 folds.

\subsection{Data-quality observations}
Two data-quality details matter for reproducibility. First, the 42 cohort stores key family in
\texttt{algorithm} (\eg, \texttt{RSA-2048}), while the 38 cohort stores the X.509 signature
algorithm in the same field (\eg, \texttt{sha256WithRSAEncryption}) and the key family in
\texttt{key\_type}. A pipeline that keys on \texttt{algorithm} changes semantics between
releases. The audit uses \texttt{key\_size} when present, which is authoritative in both
cohorts. Second, one asset in the 42 cohort and two in the 38 cohort have no key size; the
loader substitutes a family default (2048 bits for RSA). The substitution affects one pair in
the 42 cohort and does not change the ceiling conclusion. Both issues are documented in the
released audit.

does not expose another; they are \emph{mutually concealing} in the sense that a researcher who
stops at the first clean signal will miss the rest.

The modes interlock in a specific order. M2 masks M3: with a circular baseline and no message
passing, a researcher checking the graph pipeline finds a well-formed network and a decreasing
loss. M3 masks M4: small graphs with two or three distinct key sizes produce high $\tb$ that
looks like skill. M4 masks M5: a tie-saturated metric makes the vacuous top-$k$ figure look
consistent with the rest. M1 masks all of them by making every fold superficially clean. Only
recomputation from the released artifacts---not provenance metadata---breaks the chain. A
companion audit applies the same checklist to a second PQC system with markedly better artifact
hygiene (committed per-example predictions, group-level leakage audits, zero documentation
contradictions) and finds that it still fails M2, because a fixed linear model beats its
reported classifier on all three evaluation seeds~\cite{qtrust_validity_audit}. Better
provenance did not produce better validity, which is the general lesson the ceiling analysis
illustrates in one system.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Leave-one-estate-out training}
The primary artifact is the released 42-fold LOO campaign. For each fold, the model is
initialized from \texttt{model\_gpu\_v3.pt}, trained for 30 epochs on the 41 remaining real
estates plus 2,000 synthetic estates, validated on a held-out subset of the training graphs, and
evaluated on the unseen estate. The released configuration uses batch size 64, learning rate
$5\times10^{-4}$, and seed 42. The synthetic estates are generated by the same doctrine formula,
so the training mixture is doctrine-supervised in two ways: the real labels are doctrine
priorities, and the synthetic labels are doctrine priorities.

\subsection{Zero-synthetic control}
To test whether the synthetic mixture drives the result, we re-ran the same 42 folds with
$n_{\text{synthetic}}=0$: 41 real estates per fold, batch size 16, 30 epochs, same
initialization and seed, CPU. This is a strict ablation of the synthetic component. We ran the
same protocol on the 38-estate cohort (37 real estates per fold). The zero-synthetic run is not
a new model class; it isolates the contribution of the synthetic data.

\subsection{Clean warm-start control}
The released checkpoint records 40 real graphs among its 100{,}000 training inputs, so the LOO
initialization has prior exposure to 40 of the 42 held-out estates. To isolate that exposure we
retrained a base checkpoint from scratch on synthetic doctrine graphs only
(\texttt{n\_real\_graphs=0}, verified post-training) and re-ran the identical 42-fold protocol
from that clean warm start; the initialization is the only deliberate difference. The run is
CPU-only by design: deterministic-algorithm mode has no CUDA kernel for the listwise loss, and
weakening determinism would defeat the purpose of a trustworthy warm start. The reduced-CPU
path caps base training at 2{,}000 graphs and 5 epochs against the released checkpoint's
100{,}000 graphs and 173 epochs, so warm start and training budget vary together; we do not
attribute the difference between them and disclose the confound.

\subsection{Transparent baselines}
We evaluated four baselines on the identical folds, targets, and metric:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Key-size rule:} sort by descending key size; parameter-free.
\item \textbf{Pairwise logistic:} six features, no intercept (seven parameters), trained on all
distinguishable pairs of the 41 training estates; predicts the probability that asset $i$
precedes $j$.
\item \textbf{Ridge:} six features, trained on pooled priority values of the 41 training
estates; predicts a continuous priority; six parameters.
\item \textbf{Zero-shot:} the released checkpoint applied to each estate without any
fold-specific fine-tuning.
\end{itemize}
The logistic and ridge models use the same six features as the GNN. They are not independent of
the doctrine---their training labels are doctrine priorities---but they test whether the GNN's
architecture and capacity are necessary to reproduce the result. The zero-shot baseline tests
whether fold-specific training is necessary. A random-permutation baseline is included for
scale.

\subsection{Metrics}
The primary metric is tie-aware $\tb$ against the target preorder, as defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:taub}. We also report the ceiling $\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$, attainment
$\tb/\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$, pooled pair accuracy (concordant distinguishable pairs divided by
$\distinct$), exact-rank agreement (fraction of folds whose full permutation equals the target's
arbitrary tie-break), node-rank accuracy (fraction of assets in their exact target position),
and top-5/top-10 set overlap. The last three are sensitive to within-tie order; we report them
to show what $\tb$ hides, not as primary evidence.

\subsection{Statistics}
For paired comparisons we report the mean difference, a 20{,}000-resample paired bootstrap 95\%
confidence interval, a 20{,}000-resample sign-flip permutation $p$-value, win/tie/loss counts,
Cohen's $d_z$, and the 80\% minimum detectable effect at the observed paired standard deviation.
We do not use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the main comparison because 41 of 42 differences
are exactly zero; the test is undefined or uninformative under ties. For the discovery
re-evaluation we report a repository-cluster bootstrap (5{,}000 resamples over the eight
evaluation source groups) to acknowledge within-repository correlation.

\subsection{The metric determines the verdict}
\label{sec:metricverdict}

Table~\ref{tab:metricverdict} applies the taxonomy to the real corpus. The doctrine ordering is
scored under four metrics, all computed from the 42 CBOMs. Goodman--Kruskal $\gamma$ is $1.000$
on every fold by construction: the doctrine has no discordant distinguishable pairs. Spearman
$\rho$ is $0.807$ (mean), but its ceiling is also $0.807$: a strict ordering cannot match the
target's average ranks within tied groups, so even a perfect ordering is penalized. $\tb$ is
$0.721$ with ceiling $0.721$, and $\tau_a$ is $0.533$ with ceiling $0.533$. The same ordering is
therefore ``perfect'' ($\gamma$), ``$72\%$'' ($\tb$), ``$81\%$'' ($\rho$), or ``$53\%$''
($\tau_a$), depending entirely on the metric. A benchmark that reports one of these numbers
without its ceiling can make a saturated ordering look like a failure or a success at will.

This table is the paper's most direct answer to the objection that the ceiling theorem is a
one-line observation. The theorem is elementary; its consequence is not. The same ordering, on
the same data, is simultaneously at the top of one scale and near the middle of another. Which
number a paper reports is a modeling choice with a large effect on the apparent result, and the
tie structure---not the model---sets the scale.

\subsection{The GNN saturates the ceiling}
The released GNN reaches mean $\tb=0.716782$ (sd $0.126$, median $0.7237$), with 41 of 42 folds
at the ceiling. Pooled pair accuracy is $401/402=99.75\%$. The single error is in
\texttt{real\_gov\_edu\_2.json}: $n=7$, $\nzero=21$, 15 tied pairs, six distinguishable pairs,
ceiling $0.5345$. The model scores $0.3563$, which corresponds to five concordant pairs and one
inversion; its pair accuracy on that estate is $5/6=83.3\%$. The inversion is a cross-group
inversion: the model places one high-key asset below a low-key asset. There is no second error
anywhere in the cohort.

The fold-level paired comparison against the doctrine is therefore degenerate: mean difference
$-0.004242$, 95\% CI $[-0.012727,0]$, sign-flip $p=1.0$, 0 wins, 41 ties, 1 loss,
$d_z=-0.154$, 80\% minimum detectable effect $0.0119$. The confidence interval is bounded above
by zero because the model can never exceed the doctrine: the doctrine is at the ceiling. A test
of superiority is not merely underpowered; it is one-sided by construction.

\subsection{Statistical resolution and power}
\label{sec:power}

The paired model--doctrine differences have standard deviation $0.0275$, giving a standard error
of $0.0042$ and an $80\%$-power minimum detectable effect of $0.0119$ at 42 folds. A power
calculation converts this into a design statement (Table~\ref{tab:power}): detecting
$\Delta=0.005$ would require 237 folds, $\Delta=0.01$ requires 59, $\Delta=0.02$ requires 15, and
$\Delta=0.05$ requires 3. The observed $|\Delta|=0.0042$ is below all of these, and the ceiling
bounds it from above: no positive effect can exist, because the doctrine is at the ceiling. The
design is therefore not underpowered in the ordinary sense; it is saturated, and no number of
additional folds could make a superiority claim possible. This is why we report the comparison
descriptively rather than as a failed significance test.

\subsection{Transparent baselines saturate too}
The key-size rule matches the doctrine on 42/42 folds, as the algebra predicts. The
seven-parameter pairwise logistic also attains the ceiling on 42/42 folds. The six-parameter
ridge reaches mean $\tb=0.716781$, 41/42 folds at the ceiling, and matches the GNN's per-fold
$\tb$ on all 42 folds, including \texttt{real\_gov\_edu\_2.json} ($0.356348$ for both). A model
with 237{,}070 parameters and a model with six parameters are indistinguishable on this
benchmark.

The zero-shot checkpoint is the exception that proves the point. Without fold-specific
fine-tuning, it reaches $0.604020$ (38 inversions, 364/402 pairs, 90.5\% pair accuracy) and only
31/42 folds at the ceiling. Fine-tuning on 41 real estates is necessary to learn the key-size
rule; but once learned, the rule is linear and parameter-free. The gap between zero-shot and
fine-tuned is $+0.1128$ $\tb$, and it is entirely a gap in learning a one-dimensional ordering.

\subsection{Removing synthetic data changes nothing}
The zero-synthetic control reproduces the full campaign's $\tb$ on every fold: mean $0.716781$
versus $0.716782$, 41/42 at ceiling, same single inversion, same failing fold.
Table~\ref{tab:zerosyn} shows what does change. Exact-rank agreement rises from 1/42 to 12/42,
node-rank accuracy from $0.314$ to $0.575$, and top-5 overlap from $0.500$ to $0.571$. Tie-aware
$\tb$ is unchanged because all of these differences are within tied key-size groups. The
synthetic mixture affects the arbitrary order inside a tie; it does not affect the weak order
that $\tb$ measures. We do not interpret the exact-rank increase as an improvement: within a
genuine tie, any order is equally valid by the target's own semantics.

\subsection{The clean warm-start control}
From the synthetic-only base, the identical protocol yields mean $\tb=0.600887$ against the
unchanged doctrine $0.721024$: paired mean difference $-0.1201$ (30 folds tied, 12 strictly
negative, 0 positive), 95\% bootstrap CI $[-0.1948,-0.0567]$, paired $t=-3.34$, $p=0.0018$,
with 80\% minimum detectable effect $0.1007$. The released-init parity is therefore partly an
artifact of initialization exposure: the exposed init closes $0.116$ of the $0.120$ gap. Two
families of rank metrics now disagree: exact-rank agreement rises from $0.024$ to $0.262$,
node-rank from $0.314$ to $0.599$, and top-5 from $0.500$ to $0.714$, while tie-aware $\tb$
falls. The exposed model reproduces the label function's coarse group structure while
scrambling strict order within groups; the clean model does the reverse. A benchmark reporting
one metric family alone would draw opposite conclusions. The structural ceiling is untouched:
the key-size rule attains the doctrine's exact metric vector from no training at all, so no
warm-start hygiene or budget can raise a learner above the corpus.

\subsection{The repacked cohort replicates the ceiling}
On the 38-estate cohort, the doctrine ceiling is $0.726632$ over 923 pairs, of which 461 are
distinguishable. The zero-synthetic GNN reaches $0.717130$ (attainment $0.984$), with 36/38
folds at the ceiling and 458/461 pairs concordant (99.35\%). The three inversions are
concentrated in two estates: \texttt{real\_gov\_edu\_0.json} (ceiling $0.6547$, model $0.4364$,
two inversions among 12 pairs) and \texttt{real\_gov\_edu\_1.json} (ceiling $0.5000$, model
$0.3571$, one inversion among seven pairs). Excluding the singleton estate (where $\tb=1$ is
vacuous), the model mean is $0.7014$ versus a ceiling of $0.7114$. The repacking changes the
estate sizes and the number of distinguishable pairs, but not the conclusion: the model is at or
one to three pairs below the ceiling.

The 38 cohort is not independent of the 42 cohort (275/275 hosts overlap), so this is a protocol
replication, not a population replication. Its value is that it rules out a packing-specific
artifact: the result survives a different partition of the same hosts into estates.

\subsection{The positive control}
The real corpus has no graph structure, so it cannot test whether the GNN can use graph
structure. We therefore built a synthetic positive control: dependency-rich estates generated
with an enterprise topology, relabeled with a target that adds a multi-hop blast-radius term to
the doctrine. The target has ceiling $0.9992$ (almost all pairs distinguishable), and the
doctrine is blind to the multi-hop term. On 60 test estates, the doctrine reaches $0.9173$, the
GNN $0.8803$, and a key-size-only rule $0.1897$; the GNN loses to the doctrine (4 wins, 1 tie,
55 losses; $p=5.2\times10^{-10}$). A longer run (400 training estates, 200 epochs) improves the
GNN to $0.8950$ but it still loses to the doctrine at $0.9188$ (15 wins, 10 ties, 75 losses;
$p=6.8\times10^{-12}$).

This control supports two conclusions and one caveat. First, the $0.72$ ceiling is a property
of the real corpus, not of the metric: with a nearly tie-free target, scores rise to
$0.88$--$0.92$ and the key-size rule collapses to $0.19$, so the metric does discriminate when
the target carries more than one signal. Second, the GNN does not exploit multi-hop graph
structure well enough to beat a doctrine that only sees direct in-degree, even when such
structure is present and labeled. The caveat is that the positive control is synthetic and the
architecture/training budget may underfit the reach term; we cannot distinguish ``the model
cannot use reach'' from ``this training run did not learn reach.'' Either way, the real corpus
provides no evidence of graph reasoning, and the benchmark cannot provide such evidence without
dependency edges.

\subsection{Discovery re-evaluation}

documented repository-disjoint split (13{,}973 files, 38 source groups; 11{,}558 train, 2{,}415

accuracy $0.9271$ (confusion 1766/89/87/473). Re-running the released checkpoint on the same
split yields precision $0.9279$, recall $0.9514$, F1 $0.9395$, accuracy $0.9060$ (confusion
1763/137/90/425). No threshold reaches $0.9525$: the best F1 is $0.9428$ at threshold $0.65$
(precision $0.9477$, recall $0.9379$). A 512-token, 4{,}096-character truncation sweep on a
label- and language-stratified subsample does not recover the gap; it slightly lowers F1
relative to the 256-token baseline ($0.8083$ vs. $0.8142$ on $n=206$; $0.8105$ vs. $0.8222$ on
$n=168$). The difference is therefore not an inference-truncation artifact. It is consistent
with the documented mismatch between the model card's earlier corpus stage and the executed
training report, but the $0.9525$ figure is not reproducible from the released checkpoint and
split.

ten files, F1 ranges from $0.9691$ for C ($n=1{,}688$) to $0.4651$ for Python ($n=64$);
Solidity is $0.7162$ ($n=275$), Swift $0.7007$ ($n=112$), Go $0.7912$ ($n=71$), C++ $0.9880$
($n=86$), and Shell $0.9333$ ($n=21$). The Java stratum has 95 files and zero positive examples,
so its F1 is undefined; the naive macro-average that counts it as zero is $0.6954$, and the
macro-average over the seven strata with both classes is $0.795$. Across all ten languages
(including JavaScript $n=1$ and Rust $n=2$), the macro-average is $0.556$. The micro-F1 is
dominated by C, which is 70\% of the evaluation set. A repository-cluster bootstrap gives a 95\%
CI of $[0.720,0.979]$ for micro-F1; the interval is wide because there are only eight source
groups. The released checkpoint's discriminative power is real (ROC AUC $0.9616$, average
precision $0.9890$), but the single-number summary overstates language generality.

Calibration is poor in the mid-range: ECE (10 bins) is $0.1557$, and the low-confidence bins are
much more accurate than their confidence (\eg, the $[0.0,0.1)$ bin has mean confidence $0.069$
and accuracy $0.886$). The model is not a calibrated probability estimator. It is, however, a
useful ranker for human triage: at 50\% coverage, F1 is $1.000$; at 70\% coverage, precision is
$0.9750$ and F1 is $0.9846$; at 90\% coverage, F1 is $0.9634$. A deployment that auto-labels the
most confident 70\% and routes the remaining 30\% to review would make far fewer errors than the
full-coverage F1 suggests. This is a selective-prediction result, not a calibration result.

Contamination is too small to explain the headline. There are 11 exact-duplicate evaluation
records ($0.455\%$) and 25 records with 5-line-shingle Jaccard $\ge0.8$ against training
($1.04\%$); 12 have Jaccard $\ge0.95$. All high-similarity files are vendored AWS-LC sources.
Removing exact duplicates changes F1 from $0.9395$ to $0.9397$; removing near-duplicates at
$\ge0.8$ changes it to $0.9393$. The exact-duplicate count is small, but the near-duplicate
count is $2.3\times$ the $0.4\%$ figure in the earlier draft; the earlier draft's $0.4\%$
counted cross-repository snippets, not all near-duplicates.

\subsection{Cross-corpus checkpoint contamination}
The contamination above is \emph{within}-split: evaluation records that duplicate the training
records of the \emph{same} checkpoint. A separate and far larger failure mode appears when
checkpoints trained on \emph{different} corpora are compared on a shared evaluation split---the
natural way to ask ``did the new model get better?''. We audited this directly. Evaluating three
released checkpoints on one fixed digest-keyed, repository-disjoint split (2{,}238 records,
1{,}779 unique \texttt{(source,path)} pairs, eight held-out repositories), we counted how many
evaluation files are the \emph{exact same path} the checkpoint was trained on. v1 (the canonical
GPU fine-tune) was trained on $87.6\%$ of the evaluation files and v3 (a CPU fine-tune on a
50-source corpus) on $92.2\%$; only v4, which held the eight repositories out by construction, is
at $0.0\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). The consequence is severe: the contaminated v1 checkpoint
scores F1 $0.9329$ on files it memorized, while the clean v4 checkpoint scores $0.8966$---a naive
reading concludes the clean model is worse, when in fact it is the only one whose number means
anything. Any ``same-eval'' comparison across corpora that does not report this overlap is
uninterpretable.

the label, not the learner.

\section{What the Tie Means}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Four readings of the tie}
The earlier draft offered four readings of the 0-wins/41-ties/1-loss result: (i) the two methods
are equivalent; (ii) the doctrine is near-optimal; (iii) the scanner-observable features have an
information ceiling; and (iv) model superiority is not measurable without expert labels. Our
results support (i) descriptively and (iv) as a statement about the benchmark, but not (ii), and
only a narrow version of (iii).

Reading (i) is true but uninformative: the GNN and the doctrine produce the same weak order on
41 folds, and the six-parameter ridge produces the same weak order on all 42. Reading (ii) is
not supported. The doctrine's other terms are constants in these estates; the benchmark never
tests whether its criticality, vendor, deadline, or dependency weights are optimal. Reading (iii)
is supported only in the sense that the \emph{scanner-observable} fields in this corpus carry one
bit of ordering information (key size). It is not a statement about what enterprise CBOMs could
carry. Reading (iv) is supported, but for a reason the earlier draft did not identify: the
ceiling is $0.721$, so the comparison has 402 effective pairs and no headroom. ``Superiority is
not measurable'' is true here because the benchmark is saturated, not because the problem is
intrinsically hard to measure.

\subsection{Label circularity}
The target is generated by the same formula that defines the doctrine baseline. This is fidelity
testing by construction, and the earlier draft acknowledges it. The new point is that fidelity
testing is still informative if the target has many distinguishable pairs: a model can fail to
reproduce a complex doctrine. Here the doctrine is one-dimensional, so fidelity testing
degenerates to a key-size sort. The circularity is not a flaw in the model; it is a flaw in the
claim that the benchmark can rank model architectures.

\subsection{Construct validity}
A CBOM is a data structure, and its usefulness for prioritization depends on which fields are
populated. In this corpus, the fields that would make the doctrine multi-factor---criticality,
vendor readiness, deadlines, dependencies---are either constant or absent. The scanner records
certificate metadata well and organizational context poorly. That is a realistic limitation of
public-endpoint scanning, but it means the benchmark's construct (``migration priority'') is
operationalized as (``key size''). A benchmark that intends to measure multi-factor
prioritization must either collect the missing fields or acknowledge that it measures a proxy.

\subsection{Benchmark repair}
The ceiling theorem suggests a concrete reporting standard for learned-ranking benchmarks with
tied targets:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Report $\nzero$, $\distinct$, $\distinct/\nzero$, and the ceiling
$\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$ alongside $\tb$.
\item Report attainment $\tb/\ceil$ and pooled pair accuracy, not only mean $\tb$.
\item Report exact-rank and top-$k$ separately, and state whether within-tie order is
meaningful. If ties are genuine, do not treat arbitrary tie-break agreement as quality.
\item Include a parameter-free baseline and a linear baseline. If a six-parameter model matches a
237K-parameter model, the benchmark is not testing architecture.
\item Audit the target's field variation. A doctrine with constant terms is a one-factor rule;
an ablation shows which terms are doing work.
\item Use dependency-rich, heterogeneous estates for graph models. Zero-edge estates cannot test
graph reasoning, no matter how many parameters the model has.
\end{enumerate}
The first three are reporting fixes; the last three are data-collection fixes. The Q-Trust release
makes the first three easy to adopt because the artifacts already contain the per-fold targets
and predictions. The eight-mode checklist of Section~\ref{sec:checklist} complements this
standard: the reporting fixes expose saturation and vacuity, while the checklist exposes the
leakage and documentation failures that a saturation analysis alone would miss.

\subsection{The marginal cost of validity}
\label{sec:marginal}

The ceiling is a diagnostic, but it also prices the remedy. Because $\tb \le
\sqrt{\distinct/\nzero}$ and $\nzero$ is fixed by the number of assets, the only way to raise the
ceiling is to raise $\distinct$ --- that is, to obtain labels that separate assets currently
tied. The question a practitioner actually asks is therefore: \emph{which assets should an expert
label first, and how many labels does a usable ceiling cost?} The answer is not the intuitive one.

\begin{theorem}[Bisection efficiency]
\label{thm:bisect}
Let a tied group of size $m$ be re-scored individually, partitioning it into subgroups of sizes
$a_1,\dots,a_j$ with $\sum_i a_i = m$. The number of newly distinguishable pairs is
\begin{equation}
\Delta \distinct = \sum_{i<j} a_i a_j = \bigl(m^2 - \textstyle\sum_i a_i^2\bigr)/2 ,
\end{equation}
which is maximised by a balanced two-way split $a=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$, $b=\lceil m/2\rceil$,
giving $\Delta \distinct = \lfloor m/2\rfloor\lceil m/2\rceil \approx m^2/4$. The cost is $m$
labels, so the efficiency per label is $\Delta\distinct/m \approx m/4$, which is
\emph{increasing in $m$}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For fixed $m$, $\sum_{i<j} a_i a_j$ is maximised when $\sum_i a_i^2$ is minimised, which for a
partition of a fixed sum occurs when the parts are as equal as possible. Hence the balanced
split, with $\Delta\distinct = \lfloor m/2\rfloor\lceil m/2\rceil \ge (m^2-1)/4$. Dividing by the
$m$ labels consumed gives a per-label yield that grows linearly in $m$.
\end{proof}

The corollary is a labelling policy that runs opposite to intuition. The cost-minimising schedule
is to \emph{repeatedly bisect the largest tied group}, not to label the assets of highest business
importance. Large homogeneous clusters --- the RSA-2048 monoculture that dominates real estates
--- are the cheapest source of discriminating power per label. We verified the balanced split by
exhaustive enumeration for every $m \in [2,24]$.

\subsection{Deployment implications}
The Q-Trust pipeline is useful for inventory and attestation. The learned ranker, on this
corpus, is a key-size sort learned through a GNN. That is not a reason to discard it: a learned
model can incorporate new fields when they become available, and the pipeline can be retrained.
It is a reason not to market the current ranker as evidence of learned multi-factor
prioritization, and not to use the 0.7168/0.7210 tie as evidence that the doctrine is
near-optimal. The honest deployment claim is narrower: the system can enumerate public TLS
assets, produce a CBOM, and order them by a doctrine that currently reduces to key size; the
attestation layer can record the resulting actions.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Internal validity.} The target is generated by the doctrine, so the evaluation is a
fidelity test. The zero-synthetic and zero-shot controls isolate the contributions of synthetic
data and fold-specific training, but they do not create an independent target. The CPU
re-execution uses a different batch size (16 vs 64) and runs the zero-synthetic variant; we did
not re-execute the full 2{,}000-synthetic campaign on CPU. The full campaign's artifact is the
primary result; the zero-synthetic run reproduces its per-fold $\tb$ to $10^{-6}$, which is
strong evidence that the synthetic component is not driving the ranking. The clean warm-start
run is CPU-capped, so warm start and training budget vary together; we do not attribute the
$-0.120$ gap between them, and we note that exact-rank metrics improve under the smaller budget,
which suggests budget is not the whole story.

\textbf{External validity.} The 277 hosts are public TLS endpoints, not enterprise inventories.
They have no internal dependencies, business criticality, or vendor contracts. The 38-estate
cohort is a repacking of the same host population, not an independent sample. The results may
not transfer to enterprise CBOMs with rich dependency graphs. The positive control suggests that
when dependencies are present the metric can discriminate, but the GNN did not beat the doctrine
in our synthetic control.

We do not claim a third independent corpus; none exists for this task, and we decline to
manufacture one. Instead we separate the two things a corpus could tell us. The \emph{ceiling}
itself is a theorem, so it needs no corpus; what a corpus can test is its \emph{practical
magnitude}. Section~\ref{sec:marginal} therefore evaluates the ceiling over a designed space
spanning four orders of magnitude in estate size and four levels of tie concentration. The result
is that the ceiling is governed by concentration rather than size, which is a stronger
generalization claim than a third ad-hoc cohort would support: it predicts the ceiling for an
estate we have never seen from two measurable quantities. The claim that remains corpus-bound is
the \emph{field audit} (zero edges, constant terms, key-size-only doctrine), and that claim is
explicitly scoped to the 42 released estates.

\textbf{Construct validity.} Tie-aware $\tb$ measures weak-order fidelity, not exact migration
order. We report exact-rank and node-rank to show what it hides, but we do not claim that
within-tie order is meaningful. The discovery F1 is a file-level label metric, not a measure of
whether the discovered cryptographic usage is exploitable. The per-language macro-average is
sensitive to the inclusion of undefined strata (Java), and the repository-cluster CI is wide
because there are only eight source groups.

\textbf{Statistical validity.} The 42 folds are small (mostly seven assets) and the target has
only 402 distinguishable pairs. The paired comparison against the doctrine is degenerate because
the doctrine is at the ceiling. The discovery re-evaluation is a single deterministic forward
pass; the cluster bootstrap captures source-group correlation but not model-training variance.
The threshold sweep is descriptive, not a held-out model selection procedure.

\textbf{Conclusion validity.} The paper's negative claim is about the benchmark, not about PQC
migration or about the Q-Trust system as a whole. A null result on this corpus does not imply
that learned ranking is impossible; it implies that this corpus and target cannot demonstrate
it. The positive control is a constructive existence proof that a non-degenerate target yields
non-degenerate scores, but it is synthetic and does not establish that the GNN is the right
architecture.

\section{Conclusion}

The Q-Trust planning study reported an honest tie: a GNN and a doctrine heuristic agree on 41 of
42 estates. This paper shows that the tie is the ceiling of the metric, not evidence of parity.
On the released corpus, 401 of 803 pairs are tied and only 402 are distinguishable, so the
maximum attainable tie-aware $\tb$ is $0.721024$---the doctrine's score. The GNN gets 401 of 402
pairs right; a six-parameter ridge model reproduces its score on every fold; a parameter-free
key-size rule and a seven-parameter logistic model attain the ceiling. The mechanism is
algebraic: constant criticality, vendor, deadline, and zero dependencies reduce the doctrine to
key-size sorting. The result survives a repacked cohort (36/38 folds at ceiling), survives the
removal of all synthetic training data, and disappears only when the target is made
non-degenerate in a synthetic positive control---where the metric does discriminate but the GNN
still fails to beat a doctrine blind to multi-hop reach. A clean warm-start control shows that
the released parity is partly initialization exposure, and the released discovery checkpoint
yields F1 $0.9395$, not $0.9525$.

The broader lesson is methodological. A tie-aware rank metric with a tied target has a ceiling;
reporting the raw value without the ceiling invites a false narrative of convergence. Benchmarks
for learned prioritization should report the number of effective pairs, the ceiling, attainment,
and a field audit, and should include parameter-free and linear baselines. They should also be
audited against the eight construct-validity failure modes, because saturation, circular
baselines, degenerate graphs, and contamination conceal one another. The Q-Trust release now
does so. Its inventory and attestation layers remain useful; its learned-ranking claim, on this
corpus, does not.

\section{Q-Trust System Details}
\label{app:system}

\textbf{Discovery.} The inspector combines language-aware static rules, AST analysis, and a
fine-tuned CodeBERTa classifier. The released checkpoint is trained on the 13{,}973-file corpus
and evaluated on the repository-disjoint split described in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The rules
layer is anchored on CryptoAPI-Bench: it flags 164 of 187 insecure files (recall $0.877$) with a
misuse precision of $164/176=0.932$. The adversarial holdout reported by the project is F1
$0.533$ on 132 constructed cases; we did not re-execute it and do not use it as evidence in this
paper.

\textbf{Planner.} The GNN is a hybrid GCN/GATv2 encoder with 237{,}070 parameters, six input
features, hidden dimension 256, embedding dimension 128, eight heads, layer normalization, and a
ListMLE ranking loss. The RL sequencer packs estates into migration batches and is evaluated by a
separate reward metric; the archived run reports reward $140.34\pm8.13$ versus doctrine $140.62$
with 2 wins, 27 ties, and 11 losses. This is a descriptive tie and is not used to support the
ranking claims.

\textbf{Attestation.} The attestation layer uses UUPS-upgradeable registries, EIP-712 typed
signatures, and a timelock; the local deployment runs on a Base Sepolia fork. The release
contains 23 Foundry test files with 215 test, invariant, and fuzz functions. The layer has not
been externally audited. We describe it for completeness; it is not evidence for the ranking
result.

\textbf{Side-channel experiments.} The release includes five liboqs captures and a
trace-disjoint detector. The leave-one-trace-out result is AUC $0.963$ on four of five holds and
$0.252$ on the ML-DSA-44 signing hold, where the clean-noise distribution is outside the
training span. These experiments are orthogonal to the ranking benchmark and are not used in this
paper's claims.

\section{Reproduction Notes}
\label{app:repro}

The CPU environment is Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.x (CPU), PyTorch Geometric 2.x, and SciPy. The
zero-synthetic 42-fold run takes about 2 minutes on a laptop CPU after imports; the 38-fold run
takes about 3 minutes. The discovery re-evaluation reuses cached per-file probabilities in
\texttt{paper/analysis/codebert\_predictions.jsonl}; a full forward pass takes about 5 minutes on
CPU at 256 tokens. The 512-token sensitivity sweep is run on a stratified subsample because
full-set inference is about 30 minutes per configuration. The exact commands and JSON artifacts
are listed in Section~\ref{sec:artifacts}.
